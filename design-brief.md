Tavern: design brief
Tavern is a browser-based tool for telling stories with an AI Narrator. The primary activity is play — writing what your character does, reading what unfolds, accumulating Beats into Scenes into Stories. The Narrator is shaped by a corpus of prompt material the user authors and configures, and Tavern's claim is that this corpus is what persists: models change, but the work of authoring how the Narrator speaks, what it knows, and what it must never do is portable across whatever comes next. Tavern keeps that corpus visible and editable when you want it, and out of the way when you don't.
User. Power user of existing prompt-craft tools — SillyTavern, NovelAI, custom system-prompt rigs. Technical, opinionated about prompts, comfortable with high information density. Wants visible control surface; rewards skepticism rather than abstracting it away.
Narrator stance. The Narrator (the LLM in its game-master role) never plays a character. It tells. The user plays; the LLM is the GM. Maintaining that wall is part of the craft, typically with a Guardrail-type Direction in the strongest prompt region.
Authored material divides along one structural axis: persistent versus retrieved. Directions are persistent — toggled in, they stay, shaping how the Narrator speaks. World is retrieved — surfaced when relevant to recent Beats, scored against the conversation, optionally Pinned for guaranteed inclusion. Both live in the Catalog, organized into types of the user's own design. Tavern seeds familiar starters — Voice, Genre, Guardrail among Directions; Character, Place, Item, Lore among World — but these are starter taxonomy, not schema. A noir Story wants Factions; a mystery wants Suspects and Clues. The user adds, renames, and deletes types as the story asks. The two Kinds (Direction, World) are the system's contribution to the ontology; everything inside them is user data.
Entries share an anatomy: a name, one or more Facets (always-on or on-cue), Cues governing when they surface, Tags for grouping, and Connections that declare brings/conflicts/requires/excludes between entries.
Stories are story sessions: name, description, an anchor, accumulated Beats, and a Setup. The anchor is the situational frame — where, when, who the player is, why they're here — placed automatically into every prompt so the Narrator always knows the ground. The Setup is the assembly bundle: which Directions are active, which World types retrieve into which Marks, what's Pinned, with what thresholds and budgets. Scenes subdivide Stories and may override the Setup with Adjustments, including a Scene-specific anchor that appends to the Story's — "the cellar at midnight" layered onto "Vandermark House, autumn 1923." Live changes during a Beat are deltas; a Promote action pushes patterns up the chain — Beat to Scene, Scene to Story.
Marks are the five regions of each assembled prompt: Opening, Context, Bridge, Recent, Closing. Position has physical effect on attention — primacy at the top, recency at the bottom. Naming the regions makes prompt assembly a placement problem rather than string concatenation.
Surfaces. Three top-level views: Catalog (authoring), Play (active Story, Beat composition, Setup adjustment), Settings (provider, behavior, embeddings, storage). A debug panel surfaces the raw HTTP request for any Beat.
Constraints. Local-first: everything in IndexedDB, API key in localStorage, no telemetry, no sync, no server. Open source. Backup and restore via JSON file. Power-user densities: tens of types per Kind, hundreds of entries per type, hundreds of Stories over time, thousands of Beats per long-running Story.
